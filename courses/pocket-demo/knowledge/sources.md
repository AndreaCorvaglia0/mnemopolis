# Sources

Every fact the learner reads, with two sources (or the one textbook that states it and a second
that agrees). Checked on 13 November 2026.

| Fact | Source 1 | Source 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Sound is compressions and rarefactions; molecules oscillate about their equilibrium positions, along the direction of travel | OpenStax University Physics 1, 17.1 ("The air molecules oscillate in simple harmonic motion about their equilibrium positions") | The Physics Classroom, *Sound is a Mechanical Wave* (each particle pushes its neighbours; the disturbance is passed along) |
| Sound needs a medium; it cannot travel through a vacuum; the bell in a jar | The Physics Classroom, *Sound is a Mechanical Wave* ("cannot travel through a vacuum"; the bell-jar demonstration) | Wikipedia, *Speed of sound* (propagation "through an elastic medium") |
| Pitch is the perception of frequency; audible range about 20 to 20 000 Hz | OpenStax College Physics 2e, 17.2 ("Perception of frequency is called pitch"; "audible range of 20 to 20,000 Hz") | The Physics Classroom, *The Speed of Sound* (frequency as vibrations per unit time) |
| v = f λ; speed nearly independent of frequency, so a higher frequency has a shorter wavelength | OpenStax College Physics 2e, 17.2 | The Physics Classroom, *The Speed of Sound* ("Speed = Wavelength • Frequency"; wavelength and frequency vary inversely) |
| Air at 20 °C: about 343 m/s (331 m/s at 0 °C) | OpenStax University Physics 1, 17.2 and table 17.1 | The Physics Classroom (331 + 0.6 T); Wikipedia (343 m/s) |
| Fresh water about 1480 m/s; steel about 5960 m/s | OpenStax University Physics 1, table 17.1 (1480; steel 5960) | Wikipedia (water 1481 m/s, 4.3 times air; iron 5120 m/s: "steel about 5960" is the textbook's value for steel, the game says "about" and "some seventeen times") |
| Helium about 965 m/s and carbon dioxide about 259 m/s at 0 °C | OpenStax University Physics 1, table 17.1 | Wikipedia (sound is faster in light gases such as helium than in heavy ones): agrees in direction, gives no number |
| Usually slowest in gases, faster in liquids, fastest in solids | The Physics Classroom, *The Speed of Sound* | Wikipedia, *Speed of sound* ("typically" slowest in gases, faster in liquids, fastest in solids) |
| Each factor of 10 in intensity is 10 dB; 90 dB vs 60 dB is 1000 times; 0 dB is the threshold of hearing | OpenStax College Physics for AP 2e, 17.3 | OpenStax Physics (high school), 14.2 Sound Intensity and Sound Level (same definition) |
| Intensity is proportional to the square of the pressure amplitude | OpenStax College Physics for AP 2e, 17.3 (I = Δp² / 2ρv) | OpenStax Physics (high school), 14.2 ("the intensity of a sound is proportional to its amplitude squared") |
| 85 dB should not be exceeded for 8-hour daily exposures without protection; 90 dB "inside a heavy truck" | OpenStax College Physics for AP 2e, 17.3 (footnote and table 17.2) | (stated as the textbook's summary of agency advice; not checked against an agency text) |
| Twice the intensity is about 3 dB | arithmetic from the definition: 10 log10(2) = 3.01 | OpenStax College Physics for AP 2e, 17.3 (a factor of five is about 7 dB: the same calculation) |

Not verified with a second source, and therefore kept vague in the game: the speed in helium
(given as "about 965 m/s at 0 °C" from one table), the exact speed in steel (alloys differ).
