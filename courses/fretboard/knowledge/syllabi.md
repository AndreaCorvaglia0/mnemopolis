# How music theory is taught to guitarists: a survey of syllabi

Written before the course was designed, to decide what goes in and in which order. Fourteen
sources of four kinds were read for their topics and their order (November 2026). Everything below
is summarised in our own words; the links are where each outline was read.

## What almost all of them agree on

1. **Pitch names and the half step come first.** Every theory source starts with naming notes and
   with the distance of one half step (one fret, one piano key); guitar sources put the notes on
   the neck at the very beginning (G1, G2, G4, G6).
2. **The major scale is the hub.** Everyone teaches it as a fixed pattern of whole and half steps
   that can start anywhere, and everyone builds later topics (keys, chords, modes) on it.
3. **Chords are stacked thirds, built from the scale.** Triads come after the scale everywhere
   except in the most pattern-first book (G5), and even there the chords are explained by the
   scale they come from.
4. **Chords in a key (numbers, Roman numerals, I IV V vi) follow the triads.** Progressions are the
   payoff, not the starting point.
5. **Modes, extensions and analysis are late.** No beginner outline puts them in the first hour.

| When do intervals appear? | Before chords: T1, T2, T4, G4 | After chords, as a separate topic: G1 (grade 5), G2 | Courses that delay them still use "a third" and "a fifth" inside the triad lesson: intervals are taught where they are needed |

| Circle of fifths | Early, with key signatures: T4, T1 | Late, as a tool for keys and chord families: G1 (grade 4), G5 vol. II | Never in the first lesson; always tied to keys |
| Pentatonic and CAGED | The pentatonic first, because it is what guitarists play: G5 | After the major scale and chords: G1, G2, G4, G6 | CAGED always comes after the open chord shapes it is named after |

## What guitar-oriented courses do differently

- **The fretboard is the staff.** Notes are places on the neck, intervals are distances in frets
  and strings, scales and chords are shapes. Nobody asks for notation.
- **Movable shapes.** Because every fret is one half step on every string, one shape gives the same
  scale or chord in any key by sliding it: guitar courses exploit this from the start (pentatonic
  boxes, barre chords, CAGED), while piano-based courses spend that time on key signatures.
- **Open chords as the starting material.** Learners already know a few open chords; the theory is
  sold as the explanation of what their fingers already do.
- **Sound before symbols, but little ear training.** Audio examples are common (G4 has 94 tracks),
  structured ear training is rare outside the general courses.
- **No physics.** Not one guitar source explains why the 12th fret is in the middle of the string
  or why a fifth sounds smooth, although the guitar shows both better than any other instrument.

## Conclusion: the sequence this course adopts, and why

The course follows the order that nearly all sources share, **notes and the half step, then the
major scale, then triads**, and puts in front of it the one thing that every guitar course leaves
out and the owner asked for: **the string itself**, because a fretted string turns "frequency ratio"
into a length the learner can see. So the path is: (1) a string and the octave as half its length;
(2) two notes together, beats, and why simple ratios sound smooth; (3) the twelve notes, the circle
that brings them home and the fretboard as a ruler of half steps; (4) the major scale as one pattern
of steps that can start anywhere; (5) the triad as stacked thirds, found inside the open E chord.
Intervals get no house of their own: following G1 and G2, the half step and the whole step are
taught where the twelve notes are, and thirds and fifths where the chord needs them; the name the
interval game keeps them practised. The circle of fifths, the minor pentatonic box and the chords
of a key are extras, as in every source that places them after scale and triads; the pentatonic
box is offered early as an extra because guitarists already play it (G5). Notation is left out, as
in all guitar sources, and CAGED and modes are named as next steps rather than taught, since no
outline places them in a first hour.
