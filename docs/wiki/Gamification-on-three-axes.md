# Gamification on three axes

## Why bother

Across controlled studies, gamified learning shows small positive effects on what people learn,
on motivation and on behaviour, and the effect on learning is the most stable of the three
(Sailer & Homner, 2020). The same analysis found that a fiction around the activity mattered
for behaviour. Two cautions shape our choices: game elements cannot rescue weak teaching
material, and expected tangible rewards can weaken intrinsic motivation while informative
feedback strengthens it (Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999).

So: a fiction, yes; rewards that are access to more learning, yes; points for their own sake, no.

## Three needs, three dynamics

Learning a subject asks three different things of a person. Each gets its own dynamic, so that
none pretends to be another.

### 1. Prove: helping them remember, and the lighthouse

*Need: to check that the idea is understood.*
The people of the town have forgotten what they know (the story: `docs/STORY.md`). Every owner
can be helped to remember: a right answer brings a piece of their memory back; a wrong one costs
some of your clarity, and the owner clings to a false memory, the very trap of the option you
chose, which you are then shown how to correct. You just answer: no tone to pick, no bet on how
sure you are, one tap per question. The final remembering is with the keeper, at the top of the
lighthouse. There is tolerance for errors, and failing costs nothing: you may try again at once,
with other questions, after being told what to look at again. (Without the story, the same rules
are a duel of eloquence and a League.)

### 2. Explore: secrets, keys, upper floors

*Need: to go further than the basics, driven by curiosity.*
Helping an owner remember earns the key to their upper floor, where the advanced side of the idea
lives, and, in the story, something of yours that should not be there.
Tools from the shop open the boulder, the thicket, the locked gate. Some buildings are hidden.

The heart of exploring is the **lock**: a safe, a computer that wants its password, a door that
asks for the word. You look at it in first person, read the clue on the sticky note, and type
the answer yourself. Nothing to choose from: you have to recall it or work it out, which is
harder than recognising it and is kept for those who went looking. A wrong answer costs nothing;
your character mutters where the answer is taught, and you walk back there. Behind a lock is
secret knowledge: an advanced lab, a document, sometimes a tool or a key.

None of this is listed anywhere; see [Curiosity and mystery](Curiosity-and-mystery).

### Depth is reached by climbing

The ground floor of every house holds the essential idea, simple even when the subject is hard,
with a picture and an interaction strong enough to stay. The complication lives upstairs, the
neighbouring ideas in the extra buildings, the hardest recall behind the locks. A course also
states its level (introductory by default), which caps how far the upper floors go.

### 3. Practise: the tall grass and the coins

*Need: to remember.*
Many small questions wait in the tall grass and with passers-by. Their level is their difficulty
and their worth. They pay coins, and coins are spent in the town shop on tools: practice buys
access to exploration, not trophies.

## How the axes feed each other

```
practise -> coins -> tools -> places opened            (Explore)
prove    -> key   -> upper floor -> advanced material  (Explore)
explore  -> harder ideas -> harder rememberings won    (Prove)
```

## What we leave out, and why

- **Leaderboards and streaks**: pressure without information.
- **Penalties for losing**: they punish the errors that teach.
- **A second currency, experience levels, inventories with statistics**: each is a number to
  explain, and the subject is the thing to explain.
- **Rewards with no link to learning**: the only things to win are understanding, access and a
  story that moves on.

## Tone

The rain and the music carry the melancholy; the owners carry the comedy. They remember in
character: one is smitten, one is offended to have been helped, one admires your mind, one insists
they never forgot anything.
