# Gamification on three axes

## Why bother

Across controlled studies, gamified learning shows small positive effects on what people learn,
on motivation and on behaviour, and the effect on learning is the most stable of the three
(Sailer & Homner, 2020). The same analysis found that a fiction around the activity mattered
for behaviour. Two cautions shape our choices: game elements cannot rescue weak teaching
material, and expected tangible rewards can weaken intrinsic motivation while informative
feedback strengthens it (Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999).

So: a fiction, yes; rewards that are access to more learning, yes; points for their own sake, no.

## Three needs, three dynamics

Learning a subject asks three different things of a person. Each gets its own dynamic, so that
none pretends to be another.

### 1. Prove: the duels and the League

*Need: to check that the idea is understood.*
Every expert in town can be challenged to a duel of eloquence: a right answer is an argument
that lowers their conviction; a wrong one costs some composure and earns a rebuttal that explains
the mistake. The League is the final duel; its champion blocks the road to the next town.
There is tolerance for errors, and losing costs nothing: an exam you may retake at once, with
other questions, after being told what to look at again.

### 2. Explore: secrets, keys, upper floors

*Need: to go further than the basics, driven by curiosity.*
Beating an owner earns the key to their upper floor, where the advanced side of the idea lives.
Tools from the shop open the boulder, the thicket, the locked gate. Some buildings are hidden.
None of this is listed anywhere; see [Curiosity and mystery](Curiosity-and-mystery).

### 3. Practise: the tall grass and the coins

*Need: to remember.*
Many small questions wait in the tall grass and with passers-by. Their level is their difficulty
and their worth. They pay coins, and coins are spent in the town shop on tools: practice buys
access to exploration, not trophies.

## How the axes feed each other

```
practise -> coins -> tools -> places opened            (Explore)
prove    -> key   -> upper floor -> advanced material  (Explore)
explore  -> harder ideas -> harder duels winnable       (Prove)
```

## What we leave out, and why

- **Leaderboards and streaks**: pressure without information.
- **Penalties for losing**: they punish the errors that teach.
- **A second currency, experience levels, inventories with statistics**: each is a number to
  explain, and the subject is the thing to explain.
- **Rewards with no link to learning**: the only things to win are understanding, access and a
  story that moves on.

## Tone

A duel of eloquence between a student and a dyer over the nature of colour should not take
itself seriously. The owners lose in character: one is smitten, one sulks that they never liked
the house anyway, one admires your mind, one insists they let you win.
