# Understanding before memory

## The claim

Memorising is the second step. The first is to understand, and understanding is something the
learner builds by acting on the idea: predicting, trying, explaining, comparing, using it
somewhere else. Only then is it worth making it stick.

## Understanding: the labs

Each house has a lab in the spirit of interactive lessons: one object, one action, one surprise,
one takeaway. The learner predicts first, then finds out on a live picture.

- Learning grows as engagement moves from passive to active to constructive (Chi & Wylie, 2014).
  A lab aims at constructive: the learner produces a prediction and an explanation, not clicks.
- Predicting before seeing the result helps (Brod, Hasselhorn & Bunge, 2018).
- Elaboration techniques used in the course design: analogies with stated limits, elaborative
  questions ("why would that be?"), explaining it in one's own words, compare and contrast,
  an application in another setting, words together with pictures.
- A caution we keep in mind: behavioural activity is not the same as thinking (Martella et al.,
  2023). A lab that can be finished without thinking is a failed lab.

## A map twice

The course is designed as a concept map (ideas and the relations between them) before it becomes
a town. The town keeps the same structure: districts are themes, houses are concepts, the order
of the house numbers is the learning path. Walking the town is rehearsing the map; opening the
map recalls the town. Building or studying concept maps helps learning (Schroeder et al., 2018),
and placing things to remember along a route is the oldest memory technique there is, with a
medium effect on recall in a meta-analysis (Twomey & Kroneisen, 2021).

## Memory: the questions

Once understood, an idea is kept by retrieving it, not by rereading it. Taking a test on
material improves later retention more than studying it again (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006), and
retrieval practice and spacing are the two study techniques rated most useful in a broad review
(Dunlosky et al., 2013). In the game this is the tall grass, the passers-by and the rematches:
many small questions, spread out, preferring what was missed.

Order matters. No question is asked about a house the learner has not been through.

## Feedback

A wrong answer is the moment of highest attention. Every wrong option in a course has a named
trap, and the opponent's reply in a duel uses it: the learner is told why that answer tempts and
why it fails (Hattie & Timperley, 2007).

## What we do not claim

The evidence for single techniques is good; the evidence for this particular combination, in a
game, is our own bet. Play-testing with real learners is on the roadmap, not behind us.
